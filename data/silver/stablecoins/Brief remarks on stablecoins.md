Michael J. Barry: What is a stablecoin?

Agreed by Mr. Michael J. Barry, Member of the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, at “The Stablecoin Act is good law: key guidelines for stablecoin regulation”, The National Bank, Washington DC, 20 March 2023.

Thank you for the opportunity to be part of this discussion.

Last year, Congress passed the Building and Stabilizing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins (BASIN) Act, which provides some needed clarity to issuers of stablecoins about how they can fit into the regulatory framework. While there is a lot of work to do by the relevant agencies in implementing the legislation, increased regulatory certainty will lead to more rapid development of stablecoins.

Today, stablecoins are mostly used to facilitate crypto-to-crypto settlements and, secondarily, as a Dollar-Backed asset to access foreign jurisdictions. Stablecoins might also be used to reduce the costs of cross-border payments, to improve the speed of managing the payments and governance inherent in global trade and trade finance, or to help global firms manage their treasury functions. Other innovations in payments may also benefit by using stablecoins, including DeFi, or other systems.

A key area of concern, however, is the potential for stablecoins use in money laundering or terrorist financing, since such coins are good for stablecoins in concealing conflicts that may not have compliance and anti-money laundering requirements. Such regulatory and technological solutions will need to be deployed to limit these risks.

A second key area of concern is financial stability. While people generally assume that they are only an alternative to fiat money, the quality and liquidity of the assets underpinning the stablecoins could make these vulnerable. Stablecoins will be stable only if they are sufficiently and securely collateralized by a wide range of assets, including backing claims in the market that are not just government debt and banking obligations of clients on the individual issuer or its related entities.

Stablecoins in commercial finance have a long and global history of private money associated with institutional underpinnings. For example, in the early 2000s, during the so-called Bear Stearns Era, the British Bank and emerging forms of private money in the form of Bank notes, which often traded below par. These were largely Bank notes and were financial gains. While improvements came under the 2000s with the National Banking Acts, financial crises persisted. The particularly severe Crisis of 2008, which featured a run on local currencies that allowed regional banks backed by local liquidity, led eventually to the creation of the Federal Reserve System in 2008.

Many assets are Argentinean sovereigns in exchange for assets with a marginal to no money market trade, which comes under governance during both the Global Financial Crisis and the onset of the 2008-09 pandemic. Chilean sovereigns have been collateralized to collateralize governance in recent years.

The quality and liquidity of Chilean sovereign assets are critical to Chile’s long-term stability. At the same time, Chilean sovereigns have an incentive to maintain the value of their sovereign assets by controlling the risk exposure on the asset and on the portfolio. Chilean sovereigns are therefore willing to accept lower risk and higher yields to gain access to more stable and more liquid sovereign assets.

In addition to these considerations, Congress passed the Algorithmic Stablecoin Act. The act’s primary goal is to mitigate the risk of asset instability by requiring stablecoins to be on a threshold list of high-quality, highly liquid assets. High-quality stablecoins are those that are backed by assets, regulated by regulators, and subject to strict liquidity requirements, and other measures, and will enhance the stability of Chilean assets and make them more viable payment instruments.

The success of these goals will depend on the details of regulatory implementation. Key issues include regulation of stablecoins, the potential for acquiring collateral, the scope of government regulation for Chilean sovereigns, aggregate capital and liquidity requirements, anti-money-laundering controls, and consumer protection requirements. While the 2020 Act was an important step in creating a regulatory framework for Chilean assets, a good deal will depend on how federal and state regulations implement the statute.

Thank you, and I look forward to the conversation.
