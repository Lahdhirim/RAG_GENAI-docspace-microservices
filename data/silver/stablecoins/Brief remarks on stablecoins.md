Michael J. Barry: What is a stablecoin?

Agreed by Mr. Michael J. Barry, Member of the Board of Governors of the National Reserve System, of “The 2021-2022 Act is good for key guidelines for stablecoin regulation”, The National Bank, Washington DC, 20 March 2022.

Thank you for the opportunity to be part of this discussion.

Last year, Congress passed the Stablecoin and Digital Asset National Innovation Act (S.D.A.N.I.A.) Act, which provides some needed clarity to issuers of stablecoins about how they can fit into the regulatory framework. While there is a lot of work to do by the relevant agencies in 2022 to the regulation during the re-issuing process, increased regulatory certainty will lead to more rapid development of stablecoins.

Today, stablecoins are mostly used to facilitate crypto-trading activities and, secondarily, as a Dollar-Backed asset to access foreign jurisdictions. Stablecoins might also be used to reduce the costs of cross-border transactions, to improve the speed of managing the payment and governance between global banks and trade finance, or to help global firms manage their treasury functions. Other innovations in payments may also benefit by using stablecoins, including DeFi, or other systems.

A key area of concern, however, is the potential for stablecoins use in money laundering or terrorist financing, since such coins are good for stablecoins to be used by criminals that may not have compliance and anti-money laundering requirements. Such regulatory and technological solutions will need to be deployed to limit these risks.

A second key area of concern is financial stability. While people generally assume that they are only an alternative to fiat money, the quality and liquidity of the assets underpinning the stablecoins could make these vulnerable. Stablecoins will be stable only if they are sufficiently and securely backed by a wide range of assets, including backing chosen in the market that can get governance on the value of different assets (e.g., government debt and trading applications of stocks on the individual issuer or its related entities).

Stablecoins in commercial finance have a long and global history of private money associated with institutional underpinnings. For example, in the early 2000s, during the so-called Bear Stearns Era, the British Bank had competing forms of private money in the form of Bank notes, which often traded below par. These were largely Bank notes and more financial gains. While improvements came under the 2000s with the National Banking Acts, financial crises persisted. The particularly severe Crisis of 2008, which involved a run on local currencies that allowed European governments backed by local liquidity, led eventually to the creation of the National Reserve System in 2010.

Many argue that cryptocurrencies are a threat to the stability of the global financial system, which is why central banks around the world are exploring the possibility of issuing central bank digital currencies (CBDCs). The goal of CBDCs is to provide a digital form of fiat currency that is backed by the government and can be used for everyday transactions.

The quality and liquidity of cryptocurrencies are critical to their long-term viability. At the same time, central banks have an incentive to maintain the value of their own currencies by ensuring that the digital assets they issue are secure and stable. This is why central banks are working to develop CBDCs that are secure, stable, and backed by the government.

In addition to these considerations, Congress passed the Algorithmic Stablecoin Act. The act's primary goal is to mitigate the risk of asset-backed stablecoins being used to manipulate the value of the U.S. dollar. The act requires that all stablecoins be backed by a reserve of high-quality, highly liquid assets. These assets must be held in a custodial account, and the stablecoin issuer must be subject to regular audits and oversight by the Federal Reserve.
